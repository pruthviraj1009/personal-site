Your event tracking is probably bloated

Product analytics setups tend to grow by accretion. Every new feature adds a few events. Every question that comes up adds a few more. Nobody removes anything, because removing feels risky and adding feels free. A couple of years later you have hundreds of events, several that mean the same thing, and a team that doesn't fully trust any of them.

At Dashverse I worked on cleaning this up. Removing redundant events improved tracking efficiency by 30% and cut associated costs by 5%. The cost saving was nice. The bigger win was that people started trusting the data again.

Why bloat is a real problem

  • Cost. Most analytics tools price by event volume. Unused events are paid for every month.
  • Confusion. When video_play, play_clicked, and playback_started all exist, different people pick different ones and get different answers.
  • Fragility. Every event is something an engineer has to keep working. The more you have, the more silently break.
  • Slower analysis. Finding the right event becomes a research project.

How to audit a tracking plan

The process is simple, but needs patience:

  1. Export everything. Every event name, its properties, its volume, and when it was last queried, if your tool tells you.
  2. Map events to questions. For each event, which decision or metric depends on it? If nobody can answer, it's a candidate for removal.
  3. Find the duplicates. Group events that describe the same user action. Pick one canonical version and plan to remove the others.
  4. Check properties, not just events. Often one event with a well-designed property can replace five separate events.
  5. Agree before deleting. Share the list with everyone who uses the data. Someone will have a dashboard you didn't know about.

Design for fewer, richer events

The pattern that holds up best is a small number of events that describe meaningful user actions, with properties that carry the detail. Instead of one event per button, track the action (content_started) and let properties say where it came from and what it was. Your dashboards get simpler and new questions rarely need new instrumentation.

Make it stick

Cleaning up once is easy. Staying clean needs a process: a written tracking plan that is the source of truth, a naming convention everyone follows, and a lightweight review before new events ship. The goal isn't to make tracking bureaucratic. It's to make the cost of adding an event visible, so it's a decision rather than a reflex.

The best analytics setup isn't the one that tracks the most. It's the one where any person on the team can answer a question and trust the result.
